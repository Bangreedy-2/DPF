How to Track Job Applications: The 6 Columns You Need

A simple job application tracker: six columns, one number to watch and a 15-minute weekly review.

Most job trackers fail in the same way: they start with 25 columns, and by week three nobody is filling them in. A tracker only helps if logging an application takes about two minutes. Here is the smallest version that still tells you what to do next.

The six columns you need

  1. Company
  2. Job title (and a link to the posting, saved as a PDF or screenshot, because postings disappear)
  3. Date applied
  4. Source (LinkedIn, company site, referral, recruiter, job board)
  5. Resume version you sent
  6. Status (applied, followed up, interview, offer, closed)

Everything else is optional. Salary range, contact name and notes are useful when you have them, but an empty optional column should never stop you logging the application.

Why those six

  • Date applied tells you when to follow up. A simple rule: follow up on day 7, check in once more on day 14, then close it.
  • Source shows where your interviews come from. After 20 to 30 applications you can count interviews per source. Many people find that referrals and direct applications beat mass applying through job boards, but your own numbers are what matter.
  • Resume version answers the question every job seeker hits in a phone screen: "which resume did I send them?" Name each version (for example "Ops-v2") and log the name.
  • Status lets you close things. An application with no reply after two follow-ups is closed, and closing it is a relief.

The one number to watch: interview rate

Interview rate is interviews divided by applications. If you sent 40 applications and got 2 interviews, your rate is 5%. Work it out per source and per resume version. If one resume version gets interviews and another does not, you know which one to keep sending. If a source has produced nothing after 15 applications, spend that time elsewhere.

A 15-minute weekly review

  1. Sort by date applied and send the follow-ups that are due.
  2. Close anything past day 14 with no reply.
  3. Count applications, replies and interviews for the week.
  4. Pick next week's target: a number of applications you can sustain, and a cap on the hours you spend. A cap matters, because job searching without a stopping point burns people out.

Spreadsheet, Notion or paper?

  • Spreadsheet (Google Sheets or Excel): best if you want formulas to work out follow-up dates and interview rates. Works on your phone.
  • Notion: best if you like a board view where each application is a card you drag from Applied to Interview, with your interview notes inside the card.
  • Paper: best if screens are where you procrastinate. A printed log on your desk gets filled in.

Pick the one you will open every day. The format matters much less than the habit.
