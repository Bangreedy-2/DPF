How to Describe Symptoms to Your Doctor So You're Heard

What to say in a short appointment: top three concerns, what the symptom stops you doing and a 0-10 scale tied to function.

Appointments are short, and it is common to leave feeling that you did not get your main point across. You do not need medical vocabulary. You need a few specific facts in the right order. This is an organizing guide, not medical advice.

Lead with your top three concerns

Decide before the visit what your three most important concerns are, and say them in the first minute: "I have three things I'd like to cover today." Doctors plan the visit around what they hear first. If the most important thing comes up as you are leaving, there is no time left for it.

Say what the symptom stops you doing

"I'm tired" is easy to pass over. What you can no longer do is much harder to dismiss, and it gives your doctor something to measure. Compare:

  • Vague: "I'm exhausted all the time."
    Specific: "I used to walk 30 minutes a day. Now I need to lie down after a 5-minute shower, four days out of seven."
  • Vague: "My brain is foggy."
    Specific: "I lose my place mid-sentence several times a day, and I've stopped driving because of it."
  • Vague: "The pain is bad."
    Specific: "It wakes me three nights a week, and I can't stand long enough to cook dinner."

Cover six facts for each symptom

  1. When it started, and whether anything happened around that time.
  2. How often it happens (days per week).
  3. How long it lasts.
  4. How bad it gets, as a usual score and a worst score.
  5. What makes it better or worse.
  6. What has changed since your last visit.

Use a 0 to 10 scale tied to what you can do

A number means more when it is linked to function. For example: 2 means you notice it but carry on, 5 means you can do essential tasks only, 8 means you are in bed and cannot do basic tasks. Give two numbers: "Usually a 4, and a 7 on the worst days, which happen about twice a week."

Bring one page, not a diary

Months of daily notes are valuable to you, but nobody can read them in a 10-minute appointment. Sum them up on one page: top three concerns, usual and worst scores, number of good days and crash days, medication changes, and anything that was not addressed last time. Hand over a copy.

If a concern gets brushed off

Stay calm and specific. These phrases help:

  • "This is stopping me from working. What could be causing it, and what would rule those causes out?"
  • "What would you expect to see if this were getting worse, and when should I come back?"
  • "Could you note in my record that we discussed this today and decided not to test?"

Write down anything that was not covered, and put it first on the list for the next visit.

This guide helps you organize your own notes. It is not medical advice. Always follow your own care team's advice, and seek urgent care for new or severe symptoms.
